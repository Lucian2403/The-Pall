# The Pall — Spoken Dialogue Standard

**Status (updated 2026-10-22): GAME-WIDE SPOKEN AND INTERACTION WRITING STANDARD.** This standard applies to every NPC conversation, quest scene, event dialogue, combat encounter and in-world spoken exchange across the whole game, not only origin tutorials. The **Guther Anvil Stage 1 Revision 02 (2026-10-22) is approved**, while new character dialogue remains subject to individual approval. Do not copy Guther's precise personality into other characters.

## User's locked writing requirements

1. **Write for the ear, not the page.** Choose words comfortable to say out loud. One main idea per sentence. Shorten lines that need too much breath.
2. **Remove presentation-style writing.** No corporate polish, marketing speech, motivational slogans, generic fantasy proclamations, fake gravitas, artificial tutorials, or pretty prose pretending to be human speech.
3. **Vary cadence according to thought.** A short sentence is for emphasis or a genuine quick response. Longer sentences are allowed when the thought needs room. Do not manufacture a short/long pattern.
4. **Be specific when evidence exists.** Use established objects, actions, names, documents, quantities and relationships rather than vague claims. **Do not invent facts, backstory, private memories, experiences, emotions, doubts, biographical details or "realistic" filler** just to decorate a line.
5. **No synthetic humanity.** No slang, typos, mannerisms, exaggerated accents, quirky tics or forced verbal imperfections added just to make dialogue look human.
   **Additional user rule (RECONFIRMED WITHOUT EXCEPTION 2026-10-22):** Write dialogue and new player-facing prose literally. **Do not use metaphors or personification.** In particular, papers, numbers, orders, records and other objects cannot promise, want, decide, accuse or otherwise act like people. If a document records a promise or an order, identify the person or office responsible: e.g. “They loaded fewer sacks than they were meant to,” not “the paper promised more coal.” Make route and mechanical descriptions concrete and causally clear. This rule applies to new dialogue and revisions across all origins; preserve already approved text unless the user explicitly requests a correction.
6. **Critique before rewriting.** Before rewriting an existing passage, identify the **specific lines** that are generic, over-polished, repetitive, corporate, awkward to speak or otherwise wrong, and explain each issue clearly.
7. **Period constraint:** The Pall is a gritty **19th-century-inspired industrial world**. Dialogue must avoid modern institutions, pop references, administrative buzzwords and borrowed modern street slang. Use plausible work vocabulary and class/power differences, but do not overstuff with faux-Victorian dialect or theatrical archaisms.
8. **References / creative direction:** take lessons about subtext, status, bargaining, interruptions and lived-in institutional speech from *The Wire*, especially the user's linked **"Game Day"** transcript (https://transcripts.foreverdreaming.org/viewtopic.php?t=42338), and humour that can arise from extended conversational pressure and the ridiculous seriousness of mundane stakes, as found in Tarantino's work. **Do not imitate scripts verbatim, lift scenes, transplant modern slang, or reproduce a director's signature verbal gimmicks.** Original voices and setting-specific humour only.

## Suggested implementation interpretation (reviewable, not a word-count law)

- **A character speaks because something is at stake.** The speaker wants work completed, a debt paid, an answer avoided, a favour granted, authority recognized or a threat obeyed. If the line serves only as player education, move information into the UI, a job brief, or natural behaviour.
- **Speech and gameplay have different jobs.** NPCs provide motive and stakes; the interface provides precise mechanical labels (Inspect docket / Match number / Stamp plate). Don't make an NPC explain buttons.
- **Subtext over theme declarations.** A foreman need not pronounce that the city oppresses the poor: a withheld wage, a refused signature or an evasive answer shows it.
- **Dry humour is often someone refusing the terms of a question.** Choose humour from status, money, risk, a work dispute or a mismatch between claim and reality. Let a funny line sit; do not attach more jokes to prove it was funny. Dark events still need tonal restraint.
- **Tone calibration (user-approved 2026-10-14, STRICT):** use **a very small, almost imperceptible amount of uncomfortable humour and interpersonal friction** where the situation earns it. One deflection, a pointed nonanswer or a little refusal to take responsibility is enough. No added routine of barbs, punchlines or theatrical cruelty; never lengthen dialogue for a humour quota. **Treat this as an editorial acceptance criterion, not an optional stylistic suggestion.** Each new scene must pass a spoken-aloud and character-motive check before it can be approved.
- **Disagreement should change the exchange.** Let one speaker withhold, push back or change subject. Replies need not be complete sentences. Avoid three polite interview questions followed by instructional answers.
- **Silence and action are valid.** A cough, a glance at a docket, or a pause may replace an explanation if the action is already grounded in the scene. Do not use mood-laden narration to prescribe emotion.
- **Class and profession affect speech, not intelligence.** A Smithing foreman is practical; a clerk precise; a matron decisive under pressure. No one has to be a theatrical cockney stereotype, a philosophical fortune-cookie machine or an endlessly witty performer.
- **Historical vocabulary is not enough.** A real nineteenth-century word can still sound like modern office shorthand in the wrong mouth. For example, **“Contracts took my objection. You haven't got a clearance.”** is not natural speech for a labourer facing a collector. Prefer describing the actual clerk and act, e.g. **“I showed the clerk both seals. He wrote down what was wrong.”** Closer attention to who is speaking than to isolated dictionary dates.
- **Limited knowledge belongs to power, not capacity.** Hired muscle, clerks and labourers may be handed only the part of a job they must perform. Let them say plainly that they were not told, without a speech on social hierarchy or turning a working-class character into a fool.
- **Swearing can occur when justified**, but not every speaker or exchange should be profane. Modern US slang is not an authentic shortcut to *The Wire*.
- **Keep major choices meaningful**, while optional opening responses may affect stance/attitude without causing unapproved skill changes, loot, money or story flags. Branching intro should lead quickly into reusable gameplay.
- **Spoken-line check:** read once aloud. If speech requires catching breath in the middle of a single idea, or if a character sounds like they are addressing an audience rather than another person, edit it.
- **No rigid max word count or symmetrical dialogue trees.** Tension, importance and context determine how long characters talk.

## Approved narrative perspective (2026-10-22)

**Default narration and interface feedback: second person.** Describe observable actions, circumstances and physical results with “you”, or use a neutral factual observation where smoother. In-character wrong-object interactions are also **second person**, not first-person internal monologues; do not assign unchosen thoughts, feelings, motives, movement or moral judgments to the protagonist.

**Spoken player dialogue: first person** when the player selects an actual speech response. NPC speech follows each NPC's established voice.

**Personal journal: first person, personal and natural.** The player character may react, complain, doubt, feel relief or embarrassment, swear occasionally and add retrospective observations in their own voice. This is a journal, not a formal incident report or a copy of the combat log. Preserve the actual choices, findings and victory/defeat; do not invent wounds, exact combat events, received payments or facts belonging to an unplayed branch. A journal opinion (“I didn't think I'd manage it”) may be subjective without needing a numerical stat. Character reputation claims (“Everybody knows how tough Flint is”) are proposed world colour only until approved as character knowledge; do not generalise unapproved claims across the setting. For reference records or system descriptions, use a neutral register rather than forcing “I”.

**Button labels:** concise imperatives naming the player's act (“Read the work paper”, “Straighten the corner”). They are not spoken dialogue and need no pronouns.

**Wrong-object feedback:** describe the unmet requirement or physical condition without an automatically committed game action, self-critical comment, joke quota or a tool acting like a person. Never use “I haven't…” for a narration/error message. Do not highlight the right object visually; readers can use approved instructions and observations to reason about the job.

**Example:**
- Narration: “You haven't checked the number on the work paper. You cannot stamp the plate correctly yet.”
- Player dialogue: “When do we get paid?”
- Journal: “I checked the work paper and set the stamps. Had to go over the number twice to be sure.” *(Only if that second check actually happened; otherwise omit it.)*
- Button: “Set the number stamps.”

This mixed convention applies **game-wide**, including all six origin tutorials, future encounters, combat descriptions, jobs, logs and location scenes. The user expressly approved it after reviewing the Stage 2 viewpoint issue. It changes prose conventions, not quest outcomes or gameplay mechanics.

## Strict object-language check — approved 2026-10-22

Before any dialogue, inspection, journal entry or error remark is accepted, check that every described action belongs to the correct agent. Only a person can decide, believe, ask, tell, accuse, complain, promise, want or intend. A hammer cannot be “ready”, a crooked stamp cannot “give trouble”, a paper cannot “tell” the player anything and reading cannot “flatten” a metal corner. Replace such phrases with literal statements of what the character did, what a visible object physically does, and the likely consequence. Ordinary physical descriptions—metal bending, a plate rocking on a bench, soot falling—are not personification. This prohibition takes precedence over creative-writing references, rhythm, dark humour and any earlier permissive guidance. The rejected Stage 2 draft prompted this explicit review gate.

## Draft review checklist

Before accepting any dialogue, ask: **What does this speaker want now? Why use these words on this person? What is hidden rather than explained? Does the next answer actually respond? Could a shorter exchange do the same work? Would a real speaker say it aloud? Is the humour earned by the situation, or has a joke been stapled on? Is every concrete detail supported by locked world/game documents?**

### Millhand — Guther Anvil (Smithing Foreman)

**WORK**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What other work comes through here?”  
**GUTHER ANVIL:** “Council plates today. Repairs another day. The benches don't stand empty for long. Getting the council to sign for the work, that's another matter.”

**CITY**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What's work like in Grand Cylinder City?”  
**GUTHER ANVIL:** “There's heat and power from the Grand Cylinder, and plenty that needs making or mending. Doesn't mean the men doing it get paid when they ought to.”

**LORE**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What do you know about the Plume?”  
**GUTHER ANVIL:** “There were ruptures. Bad air, dust, people driven out of their homes. That's what I've been told. I can't tell you why it happened.”

### Bellrunner — Hector D. Smoke (Dispatch Master)

**WORK**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What do you handle at the Dispatch House?”  
**HECTOR D. SMOKE:** “I sort the rounds and record when the runners take them. The notices go out, the receiving marks come back. If something's late, I want to know where it was held.”

**CITY**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Does every part of the city send notices here?”  
**HECTOR D. SMOKE:** “Not every part. We've enough with shift notices and the rounds given to this house. A workshop changes its hours, somebody has to tell the workers.”

**LORE**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Do the runners go into the Pall?”  
**HECTOR D. SMOKE:** “Not on these rounds. The roads beyond the protected land get harder to follow. I deal with routes I can send somebody along and check when they return.”

### Squatter — Benny Ben (Herb Dealer)

**WORK**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Why are you so particular about the herbs?”  
**BENNY BEN:** “Because I want some growing next year. Take every stem and root now, and what do you expect me to sell when the next season comes?”

**CITY**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Where can plants still grow near the city?”  
**BENNY BEN:** “Out in the protected land there are farms and little patches that people haven't cleared. Some herbs still grow there. We ought to look after them.”

**LORE**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What about the living things farther out?”  
**BENNY BEN:** “I worry about them. The plants and animals can't do anything about what happened to the land. I don't know what's survived beyond the Grey Marches, but I hope some of it has.”

### Stitcher — Claudia Cyka (Matron)

**WORK**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What do you expect from someone working here?”  
**CLAUDIA CYKA:** “Change the dressings properly. Watch the wounds. Tell me when something is wrong. I can examine the patient, but I can't do your work and mine.”

**CITY**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Are the other city infirmaries like this one?”  
**CLAUDIA CYKA:** “I don't run the other infirmaries. Here, we have patients to attend and not enough clean linen. If you'd rather compare buildings, do it after the ward round.”

**LORE**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Can you tell when an illness comes from the Pall?”  
**CLAUDIA CYKA:** “I can examine a patient. I can tell you what I find. I'm not putting a cause to it merely because somebody mentions the Pall.”

### Scrapper — Ignatius Smog (Yard Foreman)

**WORK**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What's worth keeping from old machinery?”  
**IGNATIUS SMOG:** “Good metal. Plates, fittings, anything we can use again. The broken pieces go on the heap. Don't mix the two, or I'll have to sort it myself.”

**CITY**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Does the city send much machinery to this yard?”  
**IGNATIUS SMOG:** “Enough old iron to keep us busy. Pumps, stands, broken fittings. People still need the metal once the machines are finished with.”

**LORE**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What do you know about the land beyond the walls?”  
**IGNATIUS SMOG:** “Not enough to tell you where to walk. I've got machines to strip here. Ask somebody who makes those journeys, not a man standing in a scrap yard.”

### Inkbound — Erwin Buro (Senior Copy Clerk)

**WORK**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What does a senior copying clerk do?”  
**ERWIN BURO:** “I check that the books are copied properly and that the work is fit to send out. A mistake costs paper, and paper costs money. That's rather more than merely writing neatly.”

**CITY**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “What do you think of the city's offices?”  
**ERWIN BURO:** “Some have fine rooms and proper furnishings. Ours is rather more modest. Still, the books must be right. Respectability isn't a matter of having a grand desk.”

**LORE**  
**PLAYER CHOICE:** “Do the records explain what caused the Plume?”  
**ERWIN BURO:** “I've heard several explanations. None that I'd care to enter in a proper record without proof. We have quite enough doubtful figures to deal with already.”
